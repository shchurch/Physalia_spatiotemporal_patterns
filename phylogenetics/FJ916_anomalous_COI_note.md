# Eight anomalous COI sequences in the FJ916 series

**Status: unresolved.** This note records what was observed and what remains
untested. It does not reach a conclusion about cause.

## The observation

In our COI tree, eight GenBank sequences form a group of their own, outside
every named *Physalia* species:

`FJ916959`, `FJ916962`, `FJ916963`, `FJ916964`, `FJ916971`, `FJ916972`,
`FJ916973`, `FJ916974`

All eight come from a single 2009 submission of New Zealand material
(*Physalia* sp. DRP-2009), and all eight have unusually long branches. The same
specimens were also sequenced for the nuclear marker ITS, where they fall
within *P. megalista* — so the two markers disagree.

Nothing in our own sampling, or in any previous study we are aware of, suggests
a distinct lineage should exist here. That prompted us to ask whether these
sequences are reliable.

## What we checked

**They are not simply low-quality.** Two of the eight are badly degraded — 25%
and 51% of their bases could not be read. The other six are cleanly read, with
almost no unreadable positions, and they still group apart.

**They are not a bad batch.** The same 2009 submission contains 46 other COI
sequences, which fall normally within *P. minuta* and *P. utriculus*. Those 46
are as clean as our own modern sequences. Whatever affected these eight did not
affect the rest of the submission.

**They share a common signal.** The eight are not each independently odd. They
share 20 specific differences from normal *Physalia* COI, and each sequence
carries 16 to 19 of those 20 — including two sequences only ~200 bases long.
Random sequencing error cannot produce that. Something they have in common is
real.

**They are also individually noisy.** On top of the shared differences, each
sequence carries about five times more one-off differences than the other
sequences in the same submission. This holds even after setting the two
degraded sequences aside.

**They are not chimeric.** Identity to *Physalia* is uniformly depressed along
the whole length, rather than switching partway, which is what a spliced or
mis-assembled sequence would show.

**They are not a mixture of two amplified templates.** We tested this
explicitly. When two DNA templates amplify together, sequencers record
two-letter ambiguity codes at the positions where they differ. Across all eight
sequences there are 405 unreadable bases and **zero** such codes.

**They are not contamination from a distant organism.** A database search
returns *Physalia* as the closest match for all of them, at about 90% identity.
The next-closest organisms are unrelated hydrozoans at much lower identity,
which is ordinary background.

**They do not match any one species.** Normal sequences from this submission
match their own species at 99% and other *Physalia* at 87–90%. These eight sit
at 82–91% from *all four* species, with none standing out.

**They do not appear in modern material.** None of the 199 *Physalia* specimens
sequenced for this study carries this pattern, including specimens collected at
the same New Zealand localities.

## On the apparent signature of a functioning gene

Of the 20 shared differences, 18 do not change the encoded protein. Taken alone
that suggests a gene under natural selection, and therefore a genuine
mitochondrial sequence.

We do not think that inference is safe. In a gene of this composition, changes
at the third position of a codon are silent about 67% of the time by chance.
Any process that concentrates change at third positions — including a nuclear
copy of the gene, or comparison against a different but equally functional
version of it — produces a similar result without selection acting on these
sequences at all. Against that baseline the observed value is only modestly
elevated.

## What we cannot yet distinguish

Two possibilities remain open, and our data do not separate them:

1. **A nuclear copy of the mitochondrial gene.** Nuclear genomes commonly
   contain old insertions of mitochondrial DNA. These can be amplified in place
   of the true mitochondrial target, especially from degraded material, and
   typically exist as several slightly different copies — which would account
   for both the shared signal and the individual variation.

2. **A genuine but unsampled mitochondrial lineage.** The sequences are roughly
   as different from each *Physalia* species as those species are from one
   another, which is not implausible for a real lineage. Its complete absence
   from modern sampling would then need explaining.

The test that would separate these is to compare the sequences against the
*Physalia* nuclear genome. A nuclear copy will match a chromosome more closely
than it matches the mitochondrion. That comparison has not been run.

## Practical consequence

Regardless of cause, these eight sequences are a single 2009 submission, are
not represented in any modern sampling, disagree with the nuclear marker from
the same specimens, and include two sequences that are largely unreadable. We
therefore exclude them from the COI figure and note them here. We do not claim
to know what they are.
